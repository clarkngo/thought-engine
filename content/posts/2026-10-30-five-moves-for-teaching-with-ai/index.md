AI in a course can lower exam scores or double learning gains. The evidence says the difference is design, not the model.

Five moves any instructor can make next term:

1. **Hints, not answers.** A ChatGPT-style assistant cut exam scores 17%; the guardrailed tutor in the same trial didn't ([Bastani et al., PNAS 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)).
2. **Sequence the work.** Harvard's instructor-built tutor walked students one step at a time and more than doubled median learning gains over an active-learning class ([Kestin et al., Scientific Reports 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)).
3. **Assess without AI at least once.** It's how you see what stuck.
4. **Grade the explanation, not the artifact.** One CS1 course added weekly oral code reviews; AI use rose and exam averages held steady ([Fowles et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21374)).
5. **Teach verification as a skill.** A 2026 report from 23 computing educators says verifying AI-generated work is gaining ground as implementation-level coding loses it ([Lee et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07545)).

Why it matters: in the [Digital Education Council's 2026 survey](https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com), only 5% of students with AI in their courses say it transformed how they learn. Adding AI isn't redesigning.
