Students with ChatGPT-style help scored 48% higher in practice. Then 17% lower on the exam.

That's the result of a [randomized field experiment published in PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122). Nearly 1,000 high school math students got one of three things: no AI, a standard ChatGPT-style assistant, or a "tutor" version built with guardrails (it gave hints instead of answers, with teacher input).

During practice, AI looked like a miracle. The ChatGPT-style group scored 48% higher than the no-AI group. The tutor group scored 127% higher.

Then the AI was taken away for the exam. Students who had practiced with the ChatGPT-style assistant scored 17% *below* students who never had AI. The tutor group's drop essentially disappeared, but it didn't beat the no-AI group either.

The mechanism is simple. Practice is supposed to build the skill. When the tool does the thinking, the score goes up and the skill doesn't. Performance is not learning.

This isn't a high school problem. In the [Digital Education Council's 2026 global survey](https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com) of more than 27,000 university students, 19% said they are retaining less and 22% said they find it harder to work without AI. And [Lumina–Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/704090/routine-college-students-despite-campus-limits.aspx) finds 57% of U.S. college students already use AI in coursework every week. In most courses, the default is the ChatGPT-style arm.

One honest caveat: this was one trial, in one subject, in one country. And notice what even the best design achieved: it prevented harm, but it didn't produce a gain. Guardrails are the floor, not the finish line.

**Design for learning, not performance:**

- **Tutors give hints, not answers.** If you deploy or recommend an AI tool, configure it to scaffold, not solve.
- **Assess without AI at least once.** Otherwise you can't see what actually stuck.
- **Grade the reasoning, not just the output.** Ask students to explain, defend, or extend the work the AI helped with.

If your students' homework scores went up this year, how do you know they learned more?
