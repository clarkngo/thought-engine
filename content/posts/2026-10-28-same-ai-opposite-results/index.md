Two randomized trials. The same kind of AI. Opposite results.

**Trial one** ([Bastani et al., PNAS 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)): nearly 1,000 high school math students. Those who practiced with a ChatGPT-style assistant scored 17% *lower* on the exam than students who never had AI.

**Trial two** ([Kestin et al., Scientific Reports 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)): 194 Harvard undergraduates in introductory physics. Students using a structured AI tutor at home achieved a median learning gain more than double that of an in-class active-learning lesson, in less time (a median of 49 minutes against a 60-minute class).

Same technology. So what changed?

The design. The Harvard tutor was built by the instructors: prompts encoding active engagement and cognitive load, a platform that walked students through each part of each problem one step at a time, and expert-written step-by-step solutions so the AI stayed accurate. The PNAS assistant answered whatever it was asked.

"Does AI help students learn?" is the wrong question. The right one is: *what does the AI make the student do?*

Caveats matter here. The trials differ in age, subject, and outcome measure, so the numbers can't be compared head to head. And the Harvard authors explicitly don't claim structured tutoring will always beat active learning, especially for complex synthesis.

**Design decides the direction:**

- **Sequence the work.** One step at a time, with the student doing each step.
- **Write the expert prompts and answers yourself.** Don't outsource your pedagogy to a default chatbot.
- **Measure learning without the AI,** not performance with it.
