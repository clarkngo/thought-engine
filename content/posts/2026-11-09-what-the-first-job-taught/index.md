Employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with their peers in less-exposed jobs. Experienced workers in the same jobs show no comparable gap.

That's from the [August 2026 update of Stanford's "Canaries in the Coal Mine?"](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/) (Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen), which tracks millions of U.S. workers in ADP payroll data through June 2026. The gap was 15% a year earlier. It's still widening.

Nobody fired the juniors. The decline runs through reduced hiring, not separations.

A [Harvard study of 62 million résumés](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5425555) (Hosseini & Lichtinger) finds the same shape from a different angle. After firms adopted generative AI, junior employment fell about 9% relative to non-adopters within six quarters. Senior employment showed no break. Promotion rates stayed broadly stable. The cut happened at the front door. And by the authors' ranking of schools, graduates of the middle tiers saw the steepest declines, not the most or least selective.

Here's the uncomfortable part for universities. The Stanford team's proposed mechanism: AI substitutes for **codified** knowledge, the formal kind "taught through education, textbooks, or written procedures." It complements **tacit** knowledge, built through practice, mentorship, and repeated exposure to real situations. Occupations heavy in codified knowledge show slower entry-level growth; those heavy in tacit knowledge show faster growth for experienced workers.

AI replaces what we teach. It complements what the first job taught.

The first job was never just a job. It was where graduates turned knowledge into judgment. That rung is thinning.

So should universities train students to be senior? No. Nobody skips to judgment. But we can build the reps the first job used to give.

Caveats: both studies are descriptive, not causal. The Stanford gap shrinks when controlling for occupational education levels, and the codified-knowledge gradient is not significant under that control (the tacit gradient for experienced workers survives). The Harvard authors also suggest firms may be trimming junior hiring in advance, for roles they expect to automate.

**Build the reps the first job used to give:**

- **Supervised practice on messy problems.** Not tidy problem sets AI can finish in seconds.
- **Review cycles with a human.** Feedback on real work is where tacit knowledge forms.
- **Grade judgment, not output.** Ask why this approach and not another.

If the first job stops teaching, who does?
