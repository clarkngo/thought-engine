# LinkedIn copy: AI Replaces What We Teach. It Complements What the First Job Taught.

## Post

Young workers in AI-exposed jobs: 19% below where they should be.

Experienced workers in the same jobs: no gap.

That's Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, tracking millions of U.S. payroll records through June 2026.

Nobody fired the juniors.

Firms just hired fewer of them.

A Harvard study of 62 million résumés found the same shape. After firms adopted generative AI, junior employment fell about 9% relative to other firms. Senior employment didn't break. Promotions held steady.

The cut happened at the front door.

The steepest drop? Graduates of mid-tier schools. Not the elite. Not the least selective.

Here's the uncomfortable part for universities.

Stanford's explanation: AI substitutes for codified knowledge, the kind "taught through education, textbooks, or written procedures."

It complements tacit knowledge. The kind built through practice, mentorship, and real situations.

AI replaces what we teach.

It complements what the first job taught.

The first job was never just a job. It was where graduates turned knowledge into judgment.

So should universities train students to be senior?

No. Nobody skips to judgment.

But we can build the reps the first job used to give:

• Supervised practice on messy problems. Not tidy problem sets AI finishes in seconds.
• Review cycles with a human. Feedback on real work is where tacit knowledge forms.
• Grade judgment, not output. Ask why this approach and not another.

(Caveat: both studies are descriptive, not causal.)

If the first job stops teaching, who does?

## First comment

🔗 Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 2026), "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of AI": https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/
🔗 Hosseini & Lichtinger (Harvard), "Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change": https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5425555

Notes on the numbers: 19% is Stanford's descriptive gap for ages 22–25 in the most AI-exposed jobs versus less-exposed peers (it was 15% a year earlier; earlier versions headlined 13% and 16% from regression estimates). The 9% is Harvard's estimate six quarters after 2023Q1. The school tiers are the Harvard authors' own prestige ranking. Part of the Stanford gap shrinks when controlling for education, and the Harvard authors note firms may be cutting junior hiring in advance of automation they expect.
