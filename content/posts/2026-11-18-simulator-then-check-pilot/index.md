Airline pilots train in some of the best simulators ever built. Then the rules still put a check pilot beside them for their first real flights.

That's [14 CFR 121.434](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/121.434), the U.S. rule for airline pilots' "operating experience." After ground school and simulator training, a new pilot must fly real operations under the supervision of a check pilot. For turbojet initial training (Group II), that's 25 hours and at least 4 operating cycles. Then at least 100 hours of line flying within 120 days to consolidate the skill.

The detail that matters: [Appendix H](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/appendix-H_to_part_121), the rule that lets advanced simulators replace training in the airplane, explicitly excludes this stage from simulator credit. Aviation decided the simulator can replace the practice reps. It can't replace supervised first flights.

This is the week's thread. [Monday](/posts/2026-11-16-practice-without-feedback/): practice with an AI patient alone made novice counselors less empathetic; feedback made them better. [Tuesday](/posts/2026-11-17-rehearse-the-conflict/): rehearsing a conflict with an AI, with feedback, cut escalation in a real conflict by 67%.

AI job simulators are the obvious fix for the [missing first rung](/posts/2026-11-09-what-the-first-job-taught/). But knowledge work never had a formal check pilot stage. It had the senior colleague who reviewed a junior's first memos, models, and pull requests. That senior is now the scarcest resource in the building, and more of their review time goes to checking AI output instead of coaching people.

Surgery suggests a way out. In a [randomized trial in JAMA Surgery](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.2564) (Giglio et al., 2025), 87 medical students practiced on a neurosurgical simulator. Students who got personalized feedback from a human instructor, built from the AI's error data, outperformed students taught by the AI tutor alone, in practice and on a harder, realistic task. A third group had a human who repeated the AI tutor's exact words. Its edge over the tutor was less consistent. The human helped most when they added judgment, not when they relayed the machine. (A later crossover trial from the same lab was more mixed, so treat this as one strong data point, not a settled answer.)

My read: don't build an AI check pilot. Use AI to make your seniors efficient check pilots. The AI watches every rep and flags the moments that need judgment. The senior spends their scarce time there, adds what only experience gives, and signs off.

**Build the check pilot stage:**

- **Simulator for reps, supervised work for judgment.** Don't budget one as a replacement for the other.
- **AI watches every rep; a senior coaches the flags.** Expert attention is the scarce resource. Spend it where it counts, and spot-check what the AI didn't flag.
- **A person signs off.** Readiness is a judgment someone has to own.

If AI flagged the moments that matter in a junior's work, would your seniors have time to be check pilots again?
