# LinkedIn copy: Simulator First. Then a Check Pilot.

## Post

Airline pilots train in some of the best simulators ever built.

Then the rules still put a check pilot beside them for their first real flights.

That's the FAA's "operating experience" rule for U.S. airlines.

After ground school and the simulator, a new pilot flies real operations under a check pilot. For jets: 25 hours, at least 4 flights.

Then 100 hours of line flying within 120 days to lock it in.

The detail that matters: the rule that lets simulators replace training in the airplane explicitly excludes this stage.

Aviation decided the simulator can replace the reps.

It can't replace supervised first flights.

Knowledge work never had a formal check pilot.

It had the senior colleague who reviewed a junior's first memos, models, and pull requests.

That senior is now the scarcest resource in the building. And more of their review time goes to checking AI output instead of coaching people.

Surgery suggests a way out.

In a JAMA Surgery trial, medical students on a surgical simulator did better with personalized feedback from a human instructor, built from the AI's error data, than with the AI tutor alone.

A human who just repeated the AI's words helped less consistently.

The human mattered when they added judgment. Not when they relayed the machine.

So don't build an AI check pilot.

Use AI to make your seniors efficient check pilots.

• Simulator for reps, supervised work for judgment. Don't budget one as a replacement for the other.
• AI watches every rep; a senior coaches the flags. Expert attention is the scarce resource. Spend it where it counts.
• A person signs off. Readiness is a judgment someone has to own.

If AI flagged the moments that matter in a junior's work, would your seniors have time to be check pilots again?

## First comment

🔗 14 CFR 121.434, Operating experience (U.S. airline pilots): https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/121.434
Appendix H to Part 121, Advanced Simulation (excludes 121.434 from simulator credit): https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/appendix-H_to_part_121
Giglio et al., "Artificial Intelligence–Augmented Human Instruction and Surgical Simulation Performance: A Randomized Clinical Trial," JAMA Surgery 2025: https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.2564

Notes: 25 hours and 4 operating cycles are the initial-training figures for Group II (turbojet) airplanes; "flights" in the post means operating cycles (takeoff and landing). The 100 hours within 120 days is the consolidation requirement. JAMA Surgery: 87 medical students analyzed in three groups (AI tutor alone; a resident repeating the tutor's words; a resident giving personalized feedback from the AI's error data). The personalized group beat the AI-tutor-only group on practice performance and on a realistic transfer task; the word-for-word group beat the tutor on one practice trial. A later crossover trial from the same lab was more mixed.
