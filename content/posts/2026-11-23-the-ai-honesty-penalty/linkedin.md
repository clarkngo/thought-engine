# LinkedIn copy: The AI Honesty Penalty

## Post

We tell people to be transparent about their AI use.

Then we judge them for it.

A Duke team ran four experiments with 4,439 people, published in PNAS.

People who used AI at work were rated lazier and less competent than people who got the same help without AI.

And they saw it coming. People who imagined using AI expected to be judged lazier, and were less willing to tell their manager.

A 2026 Atlassian experiment found the same thing.

961 knowledge workers rated an identical email. When the note said AI helped, the writer was 24 points less likely to be recommended for a high-visibility project.

So people hide it.

Not because they're cheating. Because honesty has a price.

Here's the part that changes the fix.

In the Duke study, the penalty depended on who was judging.

Managers who rarely used AI favored the candidate who never used it.

Managers who used AI heavily favored the one who used it daily.

Same candidates. Opposite verdicts.

A disclosure rule is only as fair as the least AI-fluent person who reads the disclosure.

That goes for a manager reading a report. And for an instructor reading a student's AI statement.

Before you require disclosure:

• Fix the judges, not the disclosure. People who judge AI-assisted work should use the tools themselves.
• Judge the work and the reasoning, not the tool. When AI fit the task, the penalty went away.
• Make disclosure safe before you make it mandatory. Otherwise you're not collecting honesty. You're collecting a list of who to penalize.

Would you tell your manager that AI helped with your last piece of work? Why or why not?

## First comment

🔗 Reif, Larrick & Soll (Duke), "Evidence of a social evaluation penalty for using AI," PNAS 2025: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2426766122
Atlassian Teamwork Lab, "New research shows honesty about AI use at work is backfiring" (June 2026): https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/new-research-shows-honesty-about-ai-use-at-work-is-backfiring

Notes: Duke: four preregistered online experiments (N = 4,439). "Lazier" compares AI help with the same help from a non-AI source. The hiring result is an interaction with the manager's own AI use; among frequent AI users (weekly or daily) the laziness penalty wasn't significant. When AI was described as useful for the task, the penalty on job fit was offset. Atlassian: 961 U.S. knowledge workers, March–April 2026, rating the same email with and without an AI-disclosure note; vendor research. Both use hypothetical colleagues, not field data.
