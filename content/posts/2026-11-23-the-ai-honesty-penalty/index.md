We tell people to be transparent about their AI use. Then we judge them for it.

A Duke team (Reif, Larrick & Soll) ran [four preregistered experiments with 4,439 people](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2426766122), published in PNAS. People who used AI at work were rated lazier, and less competent and diligent, than people who got the same help from a non-AI source. The users saw it coming: people imagining using an AI tool expected to be judged lazier and were less willing to tell their manager or colleagues.

A 2026 experiment from [Atlassian's Teamwork Lab](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/ai-at-work/new-research-shows-honesty-about-ai-use-at-work-is-backfiring) found the same thing with 961 U.S. knowledge workers. Everyone rated the identical email. When the note said AI helped, the writer was 24 percentage points less likely to be recommended for a high-visibility project.

So people hide it. Not because they're cheating, but because honesty has a price.

Here's the part that changes the fix. In the Duke study, the penalty depended on who was judging. Managers who rarely used AI favored the job candidate who never used it. Managers who used AI heavily favored the one who used it daily. Among evaluators who used AI weekly or daily, the laziness penalty wasn't significant. And when AI was clearly useful for the task, the penalty on job fit disappeared.

Atlassian found the culture version: on teams where AI use is celebrated, the laziness stigma nearly disappears.

My read: a disclosure rule is only as fair as the least AI-fluent person who reads the disclosure. That's true for a manager reading a report, and for an instructor reading a student's AI statement.

**Before you require disclosure:**

- **Fix the judges, not the disclosure.** The penalty came from evaluators who don't use AI. People who judge AI-assisted work should use the tools themselves.
- **Judge the work and the reasoning, not the tool.** When AI fit the task, the penalty on job fit went away.
- **Make disclosure safe before you make it mandatory.** Otherwise you're not collecting honesty. You're collecting a list of who to penalize.

Caveats: these are experiments with online samples and hypothetical colleagues, not field data from real workplaces. The Atlassian study is vendor research. The Duke authors expect the penalty to shift as AI use spreads.
