AI can now play the patient, the client, the customer, and the opposing counsel. That doesn't make a simulator a training program.

This week's evidence points to four moves:

1. **Feedback, not just reps.** Novice counselors who practiced with an AI patient alone got less empathetic. With AI feedback added, their core skills improved ([Louie et al., CHI 2026](/posts/2026-11-16-practice-without-feedback/)).
2. **Rehearse the hard moment.** After practicing a conflict with an AI opponent, with feedback, people used 67% fewer escalating tactics in a real conflict than a lecture group ([Shaikh et al., CHI 2024](/posts/2026-11-17-rehearse-the-conflict/)).
3. **Then a check pilot.** After the simulator, airline pilots still fly real flights with a check pilot beside them, and the rules don't let the simulator replace that stage ([14 CFR 121.434](/posts/2026-11-18-simulator-then-check-pilot/)).
4. **Measure the skill, not the hours.** Score what someone can do afterward, without the simulator. Hours inside it are not a learning outcome. (This one is my design recommendation, drawn from the studies above.)

Who plays the coach? Where this week landed: both. The AI watches every rep and flags what needs a person. A person coaches the flags and signs off ([Wednesday](/posts/2026-11-18-simulator-then-check-pilot/), [Thursday](/posts/2026-11-19-ai-check-pilot-not-detector/)).

Which of the four moves is your team or program skipping?
