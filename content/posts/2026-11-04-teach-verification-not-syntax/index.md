An intro CS course let students use AI. AI use went up. Exam scores didn't go down.

Here's what [the course](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21374) changed: every week, students sat for a short oral code review. They explained the code they submitted, whoever (or whatever) wrote it. The course also flipped the classroom to make time for those conversations.

Keystroke logs showed students pasting significantly more of their code than before, a sign of more AI use. Yet exam averages showed no statistically significant change from earlier semesters. And students were positive about the code reviews; most complaints were about scheduling.

The insight isn't "allow AI" or "ban AI." It's that the assessment moved from the artifact to the explanation. If a student can defend the code, the course has evidence of learning, whoever typed it.

The field is moving the same way. A [2026 report from 23 computing educators and practitioners](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07545) (two NUS–Google workshops) argues that implementation-level skills like coding, debugging, testing, and documentation are losing their central place, and that education should shift toward understanding and verifying AI-generated work, plus system design, abstraction, and critical evaluation.

Caveat: one course, a preprint, and averages rather than a controlled comparison. It's a design worth piloting, not a proven formula.

**Assess the explanation, not the artifact:**

- **Short oral code reviews, every week.** Five minutes with a TA scales better than another detector.
- **Teach verification as a skill.** Reading code, writing tests, spotting the plausible-but-wrong answer.
- **Re-center the fundamentals.** You can't verify what you don't understand.
