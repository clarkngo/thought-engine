64% of faculty have taken AI literacy training. Only 29% of students think their instructors can guide them on AI.

In the US and Canada, it's 17%.

That's from the [Digital Education Council's 2026 global survey](https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com): 45,398 responses from students and faculty in 35 countries.

The North American picture is the sharpest. Faculty intent to use AI in their teaching fell from 76% to 67% in a year, the lowest of any region. Only 26% of faculty there say they're excited about AI's potential, against 57% in Asia-Pacific. And 43% of US and Canadian students say they'd support an institution-wide ban.

The report's own diagnosis: most students "cannot say whether their assessments permit AI," and most faculty are "adapting on instinct, without the frameworks or institutional support to redesign with intent."

So the training is happening. It just isn't reaching the place students look: their assignments. Tool tours build faculty familiarity. Students need something else: clear rules and visible practice in the work they're graded on.

**Readiness is what students can see:**

- **Put an AI policy on every assignment,** not just the syllabus. Allowed, limited, or not at all, and why.
- **Train for course redesign, not tool tours.** Measure training by what changed in a course.
- **Show students how you use AI yourself.** Modeling is guidance.

Faculty: what did your AI training actually change in your course?
