I won't run AI detectors on my students' work. I'm going to use AI to decide whose work I ask them to explain.

Here's the plan for a course I teach next term. Students submit their code every two weeks. Most of the grade isn't for getting it to run. It's for evaluating, analyzing, and explaining what they built.

Reviewing every submission deeply, every time, isn't realistic. Without help, most students would get a score and a sentence. That's the problem from [yesterday's post](/posts/2026-11-18-simulator-then-check-pilot/): expert attention is the scarce resource.

The plan:

- **AI does the first pass on every submission.** It flags what needs a person: an explanation that doesn't match the code, a claim with no test behind it, a thin evaluation.
- **I read the flags, not everything.** That's where I add what the AI can't: why this would break at work, and what a senior engineer would do instead.
- **A flag starts a conversation, not a penalty.** "Walk me through this line." If it's your work, that takes a minute or two.

Why not a detector? Detectors are unreliable, and a verdict teaches nothing. A two-minute conversation is a check and a lesson at the same time.

It's the design from the [JAMA Surgery trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamasurg.2025.2564) I wrote about yesterday: the AI collects the error data, and a person turns it into coaching. The human helped most when they added judgment, not when they relayed the machine.

This hasn't met students yet. Would you rather be checked by a detector, or asked to explain your work for two minutes?
