Only 19% of US and Canadian students say their program feels current for an AI world. 58% of their faculty aren't worried their teaching will go out of date.

Globally the gap is smaller but points the same way: 30% of students say their program feels current; 43% of faculty aren't worried about obsolescence. That's the [Digital Education Council's 2026 global survey](https://www.digitaleducationcouncil.com).

Students' doubts are specific. Only 28% say most of their assessments reflect the work, skills, and judgement they expect to need in an AI-enabled workplace. 37% say none or only a few do.

And policy lags practice. [Lumina–Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/704090/routine-college-students-despite-campus-limits.aspx) finds 57% of U.S. college students use AI in coursework every week, while 53% say their school discourages (42%) or prohibits (11%) it.

Here's the mechanism. Faculty tend to judge currency by content: is what I teach still true? Students judge it by the job market: will this help me do the work? Both are reasonable. Only one of them is applying for jobs.

**Let the market grade the curriculum:**

- **Ask students and employers every term** whether the course feels current, not just whether it was taught well.
- **Rewrite one assessment per course** to mirror how the work is done with AI.
- **Publish what changed,** so students can see the program moving.

Who should decide whether a curriculum is current: faculty, students, or employers?
