# The missing rung: AI and entry-level jobs (research notes, Nov 5, 2026)

Clark's question: if AI is eliminating entry-level jobs, should universities train students to be senior?

## Verified against the primary PDFs

### Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence" (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, August 2026 update)
URL: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/
ADP payroll data, millions of U.S. workers, through June 2026.

- Employment of ages 22–25 in AI-exposed occupations is **19% below where it would be had it kept pace** with less-exposed peers. Experienced workers show no comparable gap. (Abstract, p. 1)
- In levels: ages 22–25 in the two most exposed quintiles **fell ~11%** (Nov 2022 → Jun 2026); the same age group in the three least-exposed quintiles **grew ~10%**. (p. 3)
- The gap widened: 15% (July 2025 data) → 19% (June 2026). Earlier headline numbers (13%, 16%) were regression estimates; the paper now emphasizes the descriptive gap. **Use 19%, not 16%.**
- Works through **reduced hiring of young workers, not increased separations**. (Fact 4)
- Declines are concentrated where AI **substitutes** for tasks; where AI **complements** workers, employment is flat or rising, especially for experienced workers. (Fact 5)
- No economy-wide displacement. (Fact 1)
- **Mechanism (the higher-ed hook):** AI "substitutes more effectively for codified knowledge—formal, standardized, documented knowledge that can be taught through education, textbooks, or written procedures—while complementing tacit knowledge acquired through practice, mentorship, and repeated exposure to real situations." (p. 19) Occupations high in codified knowledge have slower entry-level growth; high tacit knowledge → faster growth for mid-career and senior workers.
  - Caveat (fn. 21): the codified gradient is not significant once college share is controlled; the tacit gradient for experienced workers survives.
- Caveats from the authors: descriptive, not causal; attenuates when controlling for education; some divergent trends predate generative AI; stronger in ADP than in national survey benchmarks.

### Hosseini & Lichtinger, "Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change: Evidence from U.S. Résumé and Job Posting Data" (Harvard; SSRN 5425555, version Oct 27, 2025)
URL: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5425555
62 million workers, 285,000 firms, 2015–2025. Adoption = firm posts a "GenAI integrator" job.

- From 2023Q1, junior employment at adopting firms fell **~9% after six quarters** relative to non-adopters (DiD); **~10%** in the triple-difference; **~8% after eight quarters** in the event study. Senior employment showed no break. (pp. 3–4)
- Concentrated in high-exposure roles; low-exposure roles show no significant change.
- Driven by **reduced hiring**; separations fell; **promotion rates stayed broadly stable**. (p. 5)
- **U-shape by school:** juniors from tier-3 and tier-4 institutions ("strong national or regional" and "lower-tier but standard") saw the steepest declines; tiers 1, 2, 5 smaller. Not explained by exposure (exposure rises with prestige). (Fig. 7)
  - Caveat: tiers were assigned by GPT-4o-mini prompted as an academic evaluator (fn. 20). Say "by the authors' prestige ranking".
- Authors offer a forward-looking explanation: firms may cut hiring for roles they **expect** to automate, since hiring cuts are cheaper than future layoffs. So this is not necessarily current task automation.
- Adopters are 3.7% of firms but 17.3% of employment.

### Not used
- The "~22% of pre-2023 hiring volume" figure comes from secondary commentary, not this version of the paper.
- Vendor surveys (e.g., ResumeTemplates.com) don't meet the sourcing standard.
- NACE Job Outlook 2026 (AI skills in 10.5% of entry-level postings): from search summary only, not verified.

## The angle this supports
AI substitutes for what universities mainly teach (codified knowledge) and complements what jobs used to teach (tacit knowledge from practice and mentorship). The first job was where tacit knowledge got built, and firms are hiring fewer juniors. So universities can't "train seniors"; they can build the tacit layer: supervised reps, review, real situations.
